Cadences

A cadence is a point of arrival or pause. The final chords, their bass notes, and the melody's ending all shape how complete the arrival feels.

Four common types

Authentic: V-I. It sounds conclusive because dominant tension resolves to tonic. A perfect authentic cadence usually has both chords in root position, with scale degree 1 in the soprano on I.

Plagal: IV-I. Often called the “Amen” cadence, it moves from the subdominant family to tonic.

Half: any progression that ends on V. It sounds incomplete and invites continuation, as in I-V or ii-V.

Deceptive: V-vi in a major key. The dominant begins to resolve, but the expected tonic is replaced by vi.

Examples in C major

Authentic: G-B-D to C-E-G. Plagal: F-A-C to C-E-G. Half: C-E-G to G-B-D. Deceptive: G-B-D to A-C-E.

The expected dominant resolution is \(V \rightarrow I\); the deceptive turn is \(V \rightarrow vi\).

Cadences are heard in context. The same chord pair can feel more or less final depending on rhythm, melody, register, and what follows it.
