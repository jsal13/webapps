Triads and Seventh Chords

Diatonic chords are built by stacking notes that already belong to a scale. Their quality comes from the intervals between those stacked scale degrees.

Building in thirds

Start on a scale degree, then take every other note: the root, third, and fifth form a triad. Add one more skipped note to create a seventh chord.

In C major: \(1\text{-}3\text{-}5 = C\text{-}E\text{-}G\), while \(1\text{-}3\text{-}5\text{-}7 = C\text{-}E\text{-}G\text{-}B\).

The diatonic triads of C major

Example: C, D, E, F, G, A, B generate the triads C major, D minor, E minor, F major, G major, A minor, and B diminished.

In shorthand, their qualities are I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, and vii°. Uppercase means major; lowercase means minor; the degree symbol marks diminished.

Seventh chords

Adding the seventh scale-degree note produces four-note chords. For example, G-B-D-F is the diatonic seventh chord on scale degree 5 in C major: a dominant seventh, written V7.

Listening example: Compare the pull of G7 to C: the chord's B wants to rise to C and its F wants to fall to E.
