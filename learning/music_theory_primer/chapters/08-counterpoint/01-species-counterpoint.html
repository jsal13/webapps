Chapter 8, Section 1

Species Counterpoint

Species counterpoint teaches how to write one melodic line against another so that each remains independent, singable, and consonant.

First through fifth species

Counterpoint species are a gradual approach to complexity. First species uses one note against one note; second species allows two notes against one; third species introduces more rhythmic independence; fourth species emphasizes syncopation and dissonance placement; and fifth species combines these ideas in florid counterpoint.

The goal is not just to avoid mistakes, but to shape lines that move smoothly, honor the cadence, and maintain a sense of musical independence.

Common principles

Good counterpoint balances consonance with controlled dissonance. It also avoids excessive parallel motion, keeps lines in a comfortable range, and ensures each part is capable of singing on its own.

Worked example

Example: In first species, each note in the upper line should move smoothly and remain consonant with the lower line. A line that keeps primarily thirds and sixths while avoiding repeated direct fifths creates a convincing counterpoint.

Practice

Quick exercise: Write a first-species counterpoint above a C in the lower line, keeping the intervals primarily consonant and the melodic line smooth.

Quick check: Rewrite the line with more stepwise motion and then check whether the independent melodic identity of each voice remains clear.
