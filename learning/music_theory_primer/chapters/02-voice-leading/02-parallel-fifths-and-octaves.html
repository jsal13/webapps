Parallel Fifths and Octaves

Parallel perfect intervals happen when two voices move in the same direction by similar distances and remain a perfect fifth or octave apart.

Why traditional exercises avoid them

Perfect fifths and octaves strongly fuse the two voices. Repeating one through similar motion can make the lines sound less independent, as if two voices have collapsed into one. This is why common-practice part writing generally avoids them between the same pair of voices.

Example: soprano C moves to D while bass F moves to G. The interval changes from a perfect fourth to a perfect fifth, so this is not a parallel fifth. But soprano C to D over bass G to A moves from a perfect fifth to another perfect fifth: parallel fifths.

Direct and hidden parallels

Direct, or hidden, fifths and octaves occur when similar motion leads to a perfect interval, especially when the soprano leaps. They are treated more cautiously than contrary or oblique approaches. Check the style and assignment before labeling every direct fifth an error.

When they are not a problem

These conventions describe a particular historical texture, not a universal law. Parallel fifths and octaves are normal in organum, folk music, power chords, film scoring, guitar writing, and many modern styles. They can also appear between different musical layers or as an intentional color.

The traditional warning is about \(P5 \rightarrow P5\) or \(P8 \rightarrow P8\) through similar motion in the same two voices.
