Common-Chord Modulation

A common-chord modulation uses a chord that belongs to both the old and new key. Because the chord is shared, it functions as a tonal bridge between two centers.

The pivot chord

In a diatonic pivot modulation, the music first hears a chord as part of the original key, then reinterprets it in the new key. The shared chord is the pivot. For example, a chord like G major may work as V in C major and as I in G major depending on context.

The strongest pivot chords are those that are common to both keys and are placed before a cadence that confirms the new tonic. This gives the listener enough time to register the tonal shift without feeling abrupt.

Why it works

Because harmonic function is partly context-driven, the same sonority can behave differently depending on the surrounding progression. The pivot is especially convincing when it appears in a sequence, a cadence, or a phrase that supports a clear, stable arrival on the new tonic.

Common-chord modulation is often the smoothest and most natural path from one key to another, especially in tonal music that values a clear but gradual transition.
