Chromatic & Enharmonic Modulation

Some key changes are made not by sharing a pivot chord, but by altering the spelling or function of a sonority so that the listener hears a new tonic emerge.

Chromatic modulation

Chromatic modulation uses altered pitches to smooth a tonal transition. A chord that is diatonic in one key may become a leading-tone chord or a borrowed sonority in another. The harmonic content changes enough to suggest a different tonal center, but the transition can still sound gradual.

In tonal practice, composers often use chromatic lines or altered dominants to move from one key to another without explicit pivot chords. The effect can be particularly compelling when the altered chord contains the leading tone of the new key.

Enharmonic modulation

Enharmonic modulation reinterprets a pitch or chord spelling so that a sonority functions differently in the new key. A common example is treating a diminished-seventh chord as a dominant in a new key, or spelling the same pitch classes in a way that changes their harmonic role.

Because enharmonic spellings can preserve pitch content while changing function, they are a powerful way to make a modulation feel seamless and surprising. The ear hears the same tones, but the tonal interpretation changes.
