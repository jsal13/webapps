Chapter 9, Section 1

Extended Tonality & Set Theory

Twentieth-century and contemporary music often expands tonal practice into new collections, new interval structures, and new ways of organizing pitch relationships.

Extended tonality

Extended tonality includes music that still feels centered or functional, but uses altered sonorities, non-diatonic collections, and broader harmonic color. Quartal and quintal harmony are especially common in this context, creating sonorities built on fourths or fifths rather than thirds.

In such textures, tonal function can be weakened or redistributed. Instead of relying only on dominant-to-tonic motion, a composer may build continuity through intervallic motion, pedal points, or ambivalent harmonic color.

Set theory basics

Set theory organizes pitch-class collections by interval content and transposition/inversion equivalence. A pitch-class set is a collection of pitch classes regardless of octave placement, and normal form helps identify the most compact ordering of a set.

These tools are useful for understanding music that is not fully tonal but still highly structured. They help us describe relations between sonorities without assuming a dominant-to-tonic hierarchy.

Worked example

Example: A triadic cluster built on C-E-G-B creates a clear triadic orientation, while a quartal stack built on F-B-E-A emphasizes fourths and feels more open or suspended. The interval structure changes the sense of gravity even when the outer notes stay close.

Practice

Quick exercise: Compare the sonority built on thirds with a quartal stack. Notice how the interval structure changes the way the chord feels.

Quick check: Label the collection as triadic, quartal, or mixed and explain how the interval content affects its color.
