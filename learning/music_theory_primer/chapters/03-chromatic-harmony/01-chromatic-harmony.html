Chapter 3, Section 1

Secondary Dominants & Borrowed Chords

Chromatic harmony expands the tonal palette beyond diatonic motion by creating temporary emphasis on a chord or scale degree that is not the home key's tonic.

Secondary dominants

A secondary dominant is a dominant-function chord built to tonicize a non-tonic scale degree. Common examples include V/V, V/ii, and V/vi. The goal is not a key change, but a brief intensification of a local tonal center.

In a major key, V/V creates an especially clear pull to the dominant of the dominant. In practice, the dominant chord may appear in a passing or preparatory context before the chord it targets, making the tonicization feel like a short detour rather than a true modulation.

Secondary leading-tone chords

These chords borrow dominant function from a related key, often creating a stronger leading-tone pull than a standard diatonic dominant. They can suggest a brief tonal shift without settling there long enough to establish a new key.

Borrowed chords

Borrowed chords, or modal mixture, bring in pitches or chord qualities from a parallel mode. For example, a major-key composition may borrow a iv or bVI chord from the parallel minor, producing a darker or more expressive color.

Borrowed material is most convincing when it reinforces the tonal syntax already in motion. The borrowed sonority should sound like an expressive choice within the original key, not a full drift away from it.
