Game Rules

Music Theory Roman Numeral Scrabble

Setup and goal

Players use custom cards marked with Roman numerals. Each numeral represents a diatonic chord from a key.

Before shuffling, remove one I card from the deck. Shuffle the remaining cards, then place the removed I face-up in the center of the table as the fixed starting Tonic. This shared I is the first chord of the opening phrase and does not count toward any player's hand size.

  • Two players: deal 10 cards to each player.
  • Three players: deal 7 cards to each player.
  • Four players: deal 5 cards to each player.

The round ends immediately when a player has no cards left in their hand, or when the draw pile is empty and no player can make a legal play. Compare scores; the highest score wins.

Legal cadences and points

Allowed function transitions

A phrase must follow these transitions:

  • T → S
  • T → D
  • S → T
  • S → D
  • D → T

T = tonic, S = subdominant, and D = dominant.

PhrasePatternPoints
Natural CadenceT → D → T1
Plagal Cadence (“Amen”)T → S → T0
Full CadenceT → S → D → T2
Half CadenceT → D0
ApproachT → S → D0

A Half Cadence or Approach can be resolved by any player on a later turn. The resolving player scores the points for the completed phrase in the table above, plus an additional 2 points for resolving it.

Connecting perpendicular phrases

When a horizontal phrase crosses a vertical phrase, the card at the intersection must be the identical Roman numeral in both directions. The chord itself is the shared letter.

For example, an IV card can be the middle of a horizontal I–IV–V phrase and the starting card of a vertical IV–V phrase.

Optional advanced rule: Shared tone

Instead of requiring an identical chord at the intersection, allow two different chords to cross if they share at least one scale-degree tone. For example, I (1–3–5) and vi (6–1–3) can intersect because both contain scale degrees 1 and 3.

The shared-tone rule is harder to track and begins to teach voice leading rather than only function. Use it once players are comfortable with the base game.

Turn structure

The first player lays down cards to build a legal phrase connected to the fixed starting I and scores its listed points. On each turn after that, choose one of these actions:

  1. Play a phrase. Play one or more cards from your hand to extend an existing line horizontally or vertically, Scrabble-style. Your play must connect to at least one card already on the table, and every horizontal or vertical run created by the play must follow the allowed function transitions under Legal cadences and points.
  2. Draw. If you cannot make a legal play, draw one card from the deck. Your turn ends immediately; you cannot play a card on the same turn you draw.

Hands are never refilled after a successful play. Your hand only shrinks when you play cards and grows when you draw, so play moves you toward having no cards left.

Suggested progression of house rules

Introduce additional cards and rules over successive sessions as players gain confidence:

  1. Session 1: Use the Beginner deck (I, IV, V, vi only) and shared-chord intersections only.
  2. Session 2: Switch to the Standard deck by adding ii, iii, and vii°.
  3. Session 3: Add secondary dominants and borrowed chords.
  4. Session 4: Switch on the shared-tone intersection rule for extra difficulty.

Beginner deck (32 cards)

For a first play-through or younger learners, use only I (10), IV (8), V (8), and vi (6). Leave ii, iii, and vii° out of the deck.

Secondary dominants (V/V, V/ii, and V/vi) are locked to their targets: they can only be placed immediately before the named chord, respectively V, ii, or vi. This target resolution overrides the basic function graph when needed, such as V/V → V and V/ii → ii.

Card template for printing

For each blank card, write the Roman numeral large and centered, the scale degrees in small type along the bottom, and the function letter in a corner. Color the card face or add a corner sticker by function: teal for Tonic, amber for Subdominant, and coral for Dominant.

Color-coding lets players scan the table and check legality at a glance without recalculating the function of every chord on each turn.

Optional Rule: Key Signature Selection

Before starting, choose a difficulty level that matches your comfort with music theory and dice rolling.

  1. Level 1: Beginner. Choose any key signature you prefer. C major or A minor are recommended choices.
  2. Level 2a: Intermediate (Major/Minor). Roll for the key quality: even means major and odd means minor. Then choose the tonic. For example, a 4 gives major; choose A to play in A major.
  3. Level 2b: Intermediate (Tonic). Roll to determine the tonic, then choose major or minor. For example, a 3 gives C; choose minor to play in C minor.
  4. Level 3: Advanced. Combine both dice rolls for complete randomization: use one result for the tonic and the other for major or minor quality. For example, a 10 gives G for the tonic, and a 5 gives odd/minor, resulting in G minor.
  5. Level 4: Expert. Create a custom mapping for musical modes.

For Levels 2b and 3, use this tonic mapping: 1 = A; 2 = A#/Bb; 3 = C; 4 = C#/Db; 5 = D; 6 = D#/Eb; 7 = E; 8 = F; 9 = F#/Gb; 10 = G; 11 = G#/Ab; 12 = the current player's choice of key.

After choosing a level and key, play the progression on a keyboard in the selected key, following the difficulty level you chose.
